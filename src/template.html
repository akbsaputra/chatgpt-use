How Indonesia Uses ChatGPT Differently

A data story of how Indonesians work more (and chat less) with ChatGPTA data story of how Indonesians work more (and chat less) with ChatGPT

by Akbar Saputra

Keep scrolling

Two years ago, about half of what the world typed into ChatGPT was work. Today it is less than a third. People still ask it to fix an email, but more and more they ask what to cook, how to lose weight, or whether a message from a friend sounded angry. The chatbot became less of a workplace tool and more of a life assistant.

Indonesia made much less of that move. The world's work share fell from 49% to 31%. Indonesia's held near 40%, a share that has hardly changed since late 2024. And writing, which the rest of the world has been drifting away from, remains tied for the most common thing Indonesians ask ChatGPT to help with.

This story follows that difference through OpenAI Signals, the company's own usage data: a monthly sample of a million consumer messages, sorted into categories by automated classifiers and published as country-level shares from July 2024 to September 2026. Indonesia is compared throughout with the world and with the average of its Southeast Asian neighbors. The data shows what Indonesians do with ChatGPT. To explain why, this story draws on published surveys, official statistics, and news reports.

Imagine these 100 boxes are 100 messages sent from Indonesia to ChatGPT

We shrink everything Indonesia sent to ChatGPT between July and September 2026 down to one hundred messages, one box for each percent. Sort the boxes a few different ways, and you'll start to see a picture of the country's ChatGPT users from different angles.

We start by sorting them by topic. Twenty-eight messages ask for help with writing, such as drafting, editing, translating, or summarizing texts. Another twenty-eight ask for practical guidance, a category that covers how-to advice and tutoring. Fourteen messages seek some information. Ten messages involve images and media. The remaining twenty messages are personal talk, technical assistance, and other topics.

A 2025 study of ChatGPT use worldwide found the same three uses at the top, where they make up nearly 80% of conversations. What differs in Indonesia is the order: writing shares first place, and looking things up is a distant third.

Now sort the same hundred by purpose. Forty messages are part of someone's work.

That is more than everywhere else: globally, only thirty one messages are related to work, while across Indonesia's Southeast Asian neighbors, the average is thirty three messages.

Something to notice: OpenAI's automated classifiers defined "work" pretty broadly. A second label, published only for the world as a whole, separates jobs from school. By that count, twenty of the world's hundred are work and another twelve are schoolwork. This means that some of Indonesia's forty messages here may be school homework too.

Let's move on to sort them by who is writing. OpenAI does not ask users their gender. It only infers it from first names that are overwhelmingly feminine or masculine.

By that method, sixty-one of the hundred messages sent by Indonesia come from people with feminine names. Meanwhile, the world sends fifty-two messages, close to an even split.

But it was not always this even. ChatGPT's early users were disproportionately men, and the gap has narrowed sharply since, the same study found. In Indonesia it had already flipped by July 2024, when this data begins.

Lastly, let's sort them by age. Forty-four messages come from adults under 25, and eighty-one from adults under 35.

Globally, thirty of a hundred messages come from adults under 25, and thirty-seven messages on average in Southeast Asia. These are ages that users reported themselves, and anyone under 18 is left out, so the youngest users are missing from every one of these counts.

Now that we see how a hundred messages form a picture when sorted, it has to be noted that the data counts each trait separately. Thus, the data can't say whether these highlighted numbers are the same messages.

Let's dissect these numbers

Now we know that forty of Indonesia's hundred messages are part of someone's work, that sixty-one come from people with feminine first names, and that forty-four come from adults under 25. Each number sits well away from the world's, and the rest of this story digs into them one at a time. It starts with how much Indonesia uses ChatGPT at all. Then it follows the work: why Indonesia's share stayed while the world's share fell, what kind of work it is made of, and where it seems to come from. After that, it looks at the people behind these messages, as well as how are ChatGPT's young and female users changing over time, before we wrap up with one concluding change that is happening now.

Where does Indonesia rank in the use of ChatGPT?

Let's start with a sense of scale: Indonesia is the world's fourth most populous country, with about 288 million people. OpenAI has said it is one of ChatGPT's five largest markets by weekly users, and that the number tripled in the year to September 2025.

Per person, though, Indonesia is a light user. OpenAI ranks countries by messages per capita, and it publishes the order but not the counts behind it. In the third quarter of 2026 Indonesia placed 117th of 173 countries. That means 116 countries sent more messages per person, while the other 56 countries sent fewer. It has stayed between 112th and 119th since early 2025.

So this is a very large crowd in which each person sends relatively few messages. Seems not particularly special, right? Not really. As we move forward, we'll see that what sets Indonesia apart is not how much it uses ChatGPT: it is what the messages are for.

Do Indonesians still use ChatGPT for work?

Yes, and that is what makes them unusual. Almost everywhere else, work has become a smaller part of what people do with ChatGPT. That does not mean people work with it less. In the middle of 2024, work accounted for 48.9% of consumer ChatGPT messages worldwide. By the third quarter of 2026 that share stood at 30.7%. Each gray line here is one country, and nearly all of them slope the same way.

And there's a study that might explain why saying that ChatGPT is being used less for work is actually misleading. Economists at OpenAI, Duke, and Harvard documented the first half of this slide in a 2025 working paper. It turned out, people did not stop working with ChatGPT. Personal use simply grew faster, from 53% of all messages to more than 70%, reducing the work share.

Southeast Asia moved with the world. Averaged across nine of Indonesia's neighbors, the work share fell from 44.1% to 32.6%.

All nine fell, though not equally. Singapore, the region's heaviest user by far, dropped the most, from 67.7% to 42.9%. Malaysia, Brunei, and Myanmar each lost about 16 points. Thailand, Cambodia, and Laos lost about 7, and Vietnam and the Philippines lost the least, at 5 and 4.

Indonesia started out following everyone else. Its work share dropped from 45.2% in mid-2024 to 35.9% in early 2025.

Then it stopped falling. It has moved between 36% and 43% ever since, up one quarter and down the next, with no downward drift.

The rest of the world kept sliding past it. In the second half of 2024, Indonesia ranked around 78th on this measure among roughly 150 countries. Over April to September 2026 it ranked 4th of 145.

Another way to see this is to compare each country's first six months in the data with its last six. Here, every dot is a country, placed by how many points of work share it lost.

The typical country lost 14.8 points. The world as a whole lost 15.2, and Southeast Asia lost 9.8. Indonesia lost only 1.5 points. Of 138 countries with complete data, only one held its share more tightly than Indonesia.

What do Indonesians use ChatGPT for?

What kept Indonesia's work share up? Mostly one thing: writing.

In mid-2024, writing was the most common use of ChatGPT worldwide, about a third of all messages. Since then the world's writing share has fallen to 21.2%. Indonesia's has barely moved: 30.9% then, 28.5% now.

The region deserves credit here too. Southeast Asia has always written more than the world, and its average, 29.3%, is now level with Indonesia's. But the region got there by falling eight points. Indonesia got there by standing still.

Where Indonesia and Southeast Asia diverge from the world is search. Worldwide, "seeking information" grew from 16.6% to 20.1% of messages as ChatGPT became a place to look things up. In Indonesia it shrank from 17.8% to 13.8%, the eighth-lowest share among 146 countries. Its neighbors are not far above, at 14.9%.

This shows that while much of the world increasingly uses ChatGPT to look things up, in Indonesia it is still mostly a desk where people write things.

Do Indonesians ask ChatGPT, or tell it what to do?

OpenAI's classifiers also sort every message by intent. "Asking" means seeking information or advice. "Doing" means handing over a task and expecting an output, whether it's a draft, a table, or a translation. "Expressing", meanwhile, is neither: it's simply just a thought or a feeling, the way anyone would in a journal.

Among Indonesia's work messages, 53.6% are doing. That is 7th of 119 countries, against 46.5% for the world and 48.1% for the region. Expressing, at 15.7%, is the second-lowest share anywhere. Japan sits at the opposite corner of the chart, where almost three in ten work messages expressed a thought or a feeling.

The global trend runs away from doing. Its share of the world's work messages fell from 52.7% to 46.4% in two years. In Indonesia it started at 52.6% and ended at 53.5%.

In simple words, when Indonesians use ChatGPT for work, they mostly instruct it to make something. They ask for information or advice about as often as people elsewhere, and they share a thought or a feeling much less often.

What kind of work do Indonesians bring to ChatGPT?

A separate dataset breaks each country's work messages into a few hundred activities borrowed from O*NET, the U.S. Labor Department's catalog of what people do in their jobs. Editing text is among the largest in every country. It is what comes after that sets Indonesia apart.

Indonesia ranks first among 84 countries in the share of work messages spent developing research plans or methods, at about five times the median country's rate. It ranks first in presenting research, second in evaluating scholarly work and in analyzing scientific data, and third in preparing informational or teaching materials.

Together with two teaching activities, these make up 18.0% of Indonesia's work messages. The median country's figure is 9.3%, and the Southeast Asian average is 10.9%. These read like a description of campus life: planning research, reviewing scholarly work, presenting findings, or preparing lessons.

But there's a caution. The country-level classifier knows only "work" and "not work". A student drafting a thesis proposal and a lecturer preparing a syllabus can both land in the work column, and the published data cannot separate them.

Other evidence points the same way. Indonesia has more than ten million university students, and the largest field of study, with about 2.2 million of them, is education. In a May 2024 survey of 1,501 high school and university students, 86% said they used AI for assignments at least once a month, most often to summarize and to write essays.

Then there is skripsi, the long written thesis tthat Indonesian undergraduates have traditionally had to write to earn their degree. A 2023 ministerial regulation lets study programs accept a prototype or a project instead, though the ministry stressed that the thesis itself was not abolished. Teachers are part of the picture as well. In one community program in Gresik, East Java, students from Universitas Airlangga trained primary school teachers to draft their teaching modules with AI.

How old are Indonesia's ChatGPT users?

Young, for the most part. In mid-2024, adults aged 18 to 24 sent 62.9% of Indonesia's messages.

That share has since fallen to 43.8%, as it has fallen everywhere, because older users have joined. It is still well above Southeast Asia's 37.0% and the world's 30.3%.

Look at the months instead of the quarters, and a rhythm appears. In each of the three Septembers in the data, the under-25 share jumps: by 5.5 points in 2024, 2.2 in 2025, and 6.8 in 2026.

Indonesian universities usually open their academic year in the second half of August. At Universitas Gadjah Mada, for instance, lectures for the 2026 odd semester began on August 18. September is the first full month of classes.

The growth is in the next bracket up. People aged 25 to 34 went from 23.6% of Indonesia's messages to 36.8%. That is what one would expect if early student users carried the habit into their first jobs, though the data cannot confirm it.

Adults 35 and older still send fewer than one message in five. Worldwide, more than one in three ChatGPT messages were sent by this age group.

Set beside other large countries, Indonesia looks like India and Nigeria: the youngest group is the largest one. In the United States, Germany, Japan, and South Korea, as in the world as a whole, the largest group is 25 to 34. Among 141 countries with age data for the quarter, Indonesia's under-25 share ranks 22nd.

Are Indonesia's ChatGPT users mostly women?

By OpenAI's first-name classifying methods, yes. Feminine names sent 53.4% of Indonesia's messages in July 2024, the first month of data. The world was then at 42.2% and did not cross the halfway mark until May 2025. The Southeast Asian average has hovered around that mark only since late 2025.

Indonesia stood at 60.7% in the third quarter of 2026, 6th among 107 countries with complete data.

The pattern holds topic by topic. In Indonesia, feminine names lead in all seven categories, including technical help, where they send 50.3% of messages against 37.7% worldwide.

Two cautions, though. The method counts only names that are more than 95% associated with one gender, and how well that covers Indonesian names is not documented. It also describes messages, not people.

Still, the direction fits what is known about Indonesian campuses, where women were 55.9% of all university students in 2025.

But is anything changing in how Indonesians use ChatGPT?

Yes, and it is not about words. In early 2025, "creating visual designs or displays" was 2.7% of Indonesia's work messages. By the third quarter of 2026, it was 10.6%, second only to editing text. Coding or programming, once more than twice as common as design, fell from 6.1% to 3.0%.

The turn began with a product launch. OpenAI released a new image generator inside ChatGPT on March 25, 2025, and the following month images jumped from 3.9% to 10.5% of all Indonesian messages. The first wave did not last. By December 2025 images had fallen back to 4.1% of Indonesian messages. Then the share climbed again, and by the third quarter of 2026 it was back above 10%. This time work use rose with it.

This is a global shift, and Indonesia is a little ahead of it. Design stands at 7.5% in the median country and at 8.8% across the region. In both, it overtook programming at the start of 2026, just as it did in Indonesia.

So, how does Indonesia use ChatGPT differently?

Start with what the world did. In two years, the work-related share of ChatGPT messages fell from 48.9% to 30.7% worldwide, as people brought it more of their personal lives and more of their searches. Indonesia's share dipped early and then held near 40%. Of 138 countries with complete data, only one country held its share more tightly.

What held it up is writing. Writing still takes 28.5% of Indonesia's messages, against 21.2% for the world, and Indonesians at work instruct ChatGPT to do a task far more often than they ask it a question. The tasks that stand out are the ones tied heavily on campus lives: research plans, scholarly reviews, or teaching materials.

That is also where the data reaches its first limit. Its classifier knows only "work" and "not work", so a student's thesis and a lecturer's syllabus land in the same column, and nothing published can separate them. Our reading that tied this to campus lives rests on that pattern and on outside surveys, not on a count of students.

The people behind the messages point the same way. Adults under 25 send 43.8% of them, and people with feminine first names send 60.7%. Both figures come with conditions. Age covers only adults who reported one, so users under 18 are missing. Gender is inferred from first names, by a method whose fit for Indonesian names is not documented. And each trait is counted on its own. The data cannot say how many of the young users are women, or how many of either group are the ones doing the writing.

The data also leaves out some things altogether. Every figure here is a share of messages, so apart from the per-capita rank (117th of 173), nothing measures how many Indonesians use ChatGPT or how often. Only consumer accounts are included, so use through business and corporate accounts is not counted. And a single country's monthly figures are noisy. Indonesia's work share moves by about four points from month to month, which is why this story leans on quarterly and six-month averages.

For now, the difference is plain. The world's ChatGPT has become a life companion and a search engine. Indonesians use ChatGPT mostly as if it were a work desk: a place where students, teachers, and young office workers bring something unfinished and ask for help finishing it.

Whether that is a stage every country passes through, or the lasting habit of a young, studying, writing nation, is something the next two years of data will show.

Method

All charts use OpenAI Signals v3.0, released under a CC BY 4.0 license, covering July 2024 to September 2026. "World" is the global series published by OpenAI. Where no global series exists (work activities and per-capita rank), the median country is used instead. "Southeast Asia" is a simple average of Indonesia's neighbors with complete data in each file, excluding Indonesia: nine countries for work and topics, eight for age, seven for intent, six for gender, and five for work activities. It is not weighted by population or by usage. Ranks state the number of countries with complete data for the period.
